Billing Statement vs. Invoice: Key Differences & When to Send Each

MT
MD Tousif
Published Nov 15, 2026 • 5 min read

In business accounting, many business owners use the terms billing statement (or statement of account) and invoice as if they are the exact same thing. While both documents communicate financial details to a customer, they fulfill completely different functions in your accounts receivable workflow.

Sending a statement when a customer needs an invoice can delay payment by weeks. Conversely, sending multiple fragmented invoices without an aggregate monthly statement can confuse enterprise clients. Here is the clear, practical breakdown of how both documents work together.

Quick Summary Comparison

Feature Invoice Billing Statement (Statement of Account)
Primary Purpose Requests payment for a specific purchase or completed project Summarizes all account activity, payments, and unpaid balances over time
Scope Single transaction or order Multiple transactions across a specific period (e.g., month/quarter)
Issued When? Immediately after work is delivered or milestone reached At regular calendar intervals (e.g., end of month) or for collections
Itemization Detailed item descriptions, quantities, hours, taxes, rates List of invoice numbers, dates, payment credits, and aging balances
Legal Standing Formal commercial record and legally binding payment demand Informational report and reminder of outstanding obligations
Payment Terms Explicit terms (Net 15, Net 30, Due Upon Receipt) Cumulative total due (with aging: 30, 60, 90+ days overdue)

1. What is an Invoice?

An invoice is a itemized commercial billing document sent from a seller or service provider to a client. It provides granular detail about the exact services or goods delivered, the price per unit, agreed tax amounts, and specific payment deadlines.

When to issue an invoice:

  • You finish delivering a project milestone, graphic design batch, or consulting hours.
  • A contractor completes a repair, HVAC overhaul, or plumbing installation.
  • You ship products or physical goods to a customer.

2. What is a Billing Statement?

A billing statement (also known as a Statement of Account) is an ongoing financial summary of a customer’s entire account over a selected timeframe (usually 30 days). Rather than detailing individual line items, it displays a chronological timeline of:

  • Opening Balance: Amount carried over from the prior billing cycle.
  • Invoices Issued: Number, date, and original billed total for each new invoice.
  • Payments & Credits: Cash, wire, or credit card settlements logged during the month.
  • Outstanding Balance Due: Total unpaid balance across all open invoices.
  • Aging Breakdown: How much of the balance is current (0–30 days), overdue (31–60 days), or critically delinquent (61–90+ days).
💡 Did You Know?
Banks and credit card providers issue billing statements every month. Your monthly credit card statement doesn't ask you to pay for each individual coffee purchase separately; it summarizes the aggregate purchases and provides one single balance due.

When Should You Send an Invoice vs. a Billing Statement?

Send an Invoice When:

  • You are completing a standalone, one-off transaction.
  • The client’s accounting department requires an itemized document with tax ID and purchase order (PO) numbers before processing accounts payable.
  • Work has just finished and payment terms begin today (e.g., Net 30).

Send a Billing Statement When:

  • A recurring client makes multiple purchases throughout the month and prefers settling in one combined payment at month-end.
  • A client has multiple unpaid invoices and you need a polite, structured way to remind them of the total overdue balance.
  • During year-end tax reconciliation when clients request confirmation of their total spend and open balance.

How Billing Statements Prevent Overdue Debt

When an invoice goes unpaid past its due date, sending repetitive copies of the same invoice can sometimes feel adversarial. In contrast, sending an updated Statement of Account provides a clean, neutral financial snapshot that displays:

  1. All previously paid invoices (showing good faith credit history).
  2. The specific invoices that remain past due.
  3. Applicable late fees or interest calculated with a late fee calculator.

This simple habit often accelerates payment because it makes it effortless for the client’s accounts payable team to reconcile their books without asking you for missing files.
